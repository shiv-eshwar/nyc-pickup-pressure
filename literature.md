# Related work and research contribution

Two results set the bar for this project. New York City has already mapped average for-hire pickup waits by taxi zone. Researchers have also predicted a passenger’s wait before a driver is assigned. Pickup Pressure NYC asks a different operational question. At the start of an hour, which ten zones should an analyst review to cover the greatest number of serviced requests that will take at least ten minutes to reach pickup? [NYC TLC and DOT, figure 8](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/tlc/downloads/pdf/fhv_congestion_study_report.pdf) · [Wang and Wang, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09027)

| NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission and Department of Transportation, 2019 | Figure 8 of the agencies’ congestion study maps average high-volume FHV wait times by TLC taxi zone in 2018 and 2019. | The same regulator and geography give a direct precedent. Hourly forecasts of long-wait **counts** answer a question that period averages leave open. [City report, figure 8](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/tlc/downloads/pdf/fhv_congestion_study_report.pdf) |
| Faghih, Safikhani, Moghimi and Kamga, 2019 | They aggregated Uber pickups into 15-minute intervals across 27 Manhattan traffic analysis districts and compared temporal and spatiotemporal autoregressions. The response variable was pickup volume. | Local demand has a predictable spatial and temporal structure. A credible wait forecast should compete against a simple historical ranking on the same dates. [Journal of Computing in Civil Engineering](https://doi.org/10.1061/%28ASCE%29CP.1943-5487.0000825) |
| Yang, Liang and Yang, 2021 | They fitted multilevel proportional-hazards models to observed RideAustin waits from request to **driver arrival**. Longer waits were associated with distance from the central business district, among other factors. | The clock matters. NYC’s generally available timestamps measure requested pickup time through actual pickup. Its separate on-scene timestamp applies to accessible vehicles. Numerical comparisons with Austin would mix endpoints. [Transportation Research Part D](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2021.102774) · [TLC field definitions](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/tlc/downloads/pdf/data_dictionary_trip_records_hvfhs.pdf) |
| J. Wang and G. Wang, 2025 | Their study used more than 30 million Shenzhen trips to predict an individual passenger’s pre-request wait using feature interactions and XGBoost. Predictions could be made before a driver was assigned. | This is close prior work on predicting waits in advance. Our forecast is issued for every candidate zone before the next hour’s individual requests are known. [arXiv manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09027) |
| Gao and He, 2026 | They modeled request-to-boarding time using geographically weighted XGBoost. Their Shenzhen application averaged waits per hexagon per day, using a week of order data from 19 platforms. | This is a close match for the *timestamp definition* and geographic aggregation. Our new unit of evaluation is the set of ten zones selected each hour, scored by their realized long-wait burden. ## What would count as a contribution?

Consider two hypothetical zones. If one receives 1,000 serviced requests and 3% wait ten minutes, it contributes 30 long waits. A smaller zone with 250 requests and a 16% long-wait rate contributes 40. The second zone deserves the first alert under this project’s objective. A ranking based only on request volume would reverse that decision.

The empirical test is whether recent **observable** pickup history improves the share of citywide long waits captured by ten alerts over a seasonal reference on later hours. A ride requested at 08:58 and picked up at 09:12 can contribute to the eventual 08:00–09:00 label. At 09:00 its final wait is still unknown. Historical replay must honor this distinction even though the published row contains both timestamps. A tie or loss against the seasonal reference is a substantive result about the value of recent history.

The selected zones represent a concentration of observed long waits. Low-volume neighborhoods can have high wait rates and still receive few alerts. Chicago simulation work illustrates how residential geography can affect group-level wait disparities. Here, borough breakdowns describe where the project performs well or poorly. Individual discrimination, unmet demand and the effect of sending vehicles would each require additional evidence. [Cobb et al., PNAS, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2408936121)

## Search scope

The review covered city reports and papers on NYC Uber demand, realized ride-hail wait times, prediction before driver assignment, spatial variation and decision-focused ranking. Firecrawl’s paper index supplied the demand and pre-request studies. Citation expansion, publisher records, proceedings and the city’s figure and field definitions were used to check targets, observation units and timestamps. The contribution claimed here is the documented and reproducible NYC experiment.
